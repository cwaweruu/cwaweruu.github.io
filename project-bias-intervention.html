You Found Bias in Your AI System. Now What? A Practical Intervention Playbook

The Problem

Most guidance on AI fairness focuses on how to detect bias. Very little focuses on what to do once you have found it. The gap is significant: teams that identify a fairness problem often stall because the path from "we have measured a disparity" to "we have fixed it responsibly" is not documented anywhere they can find.

This project addresses the intervention question directly: given a diagnosed bias problem, what are the available technical options, when should each be used, and what trade-offs does each introduce?

The Approach

I built a structured intervention playbook organised around three stages of the ML pipeline where bias can be addressed: pre-processing (modifying training data before model training), in-processing (modifying the training objective), and post-processing (adjusting model outputs after prediction).

For each stage, the playbook documents: the types of bias each intervention targets, the fairness metrics it affects, the accuracy trade-offs it introduces, and the conditions under which it is and is not appropriate. The playbook also includes a diagnostic decision tree to help practitioners navigate from a measured disparity to a recommended intervention class.

Key Findings

Pre-processing interventions are the most transferable across model types but require access to training data and carry the risk of over-correcting in ways that introduce new disparities for unprotected groups.

Post-processing interventions are easiest to implement in deployed systems without retraining, but they operate on outputs rather than causes and can mask underlying problems rather than resolve them.

In all cases, a bias intervention that improves one fairness metric will often worsen another. Making this trade-off explicit and intentional is the core of responsible intervention design.

Impact

The playbook provides a practical decision framework for data teams facing real fairness problems under real time and resource constraints. By organising interventions by pipeline stage and making trade-offs explicit, it helps teams choose interventions that match both their technical constraints and their normative priorities.

What I Learned

The most common mistake in bias mitigation is treating it as a purely technical problem. Every intervention embeds a value judgement about which group's outcomes matter more, and those judgements need to be made explicitly by people with domain and ethical knowledge, not implicitly by whoever wrote the correction code.
