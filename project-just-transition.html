Modelling the Social Cost of Inaction: EU Just Transition Directive Scenarios to 2045

The Problem

The European Economic and Social Committee (EESC) needed a quantitative evidence base for assessing the social cost of failing to implement the EU Just Transition Directive (JTD) ambitiously. The question was not whether the green transition would cause disruption, but what the comparative cost of weak versus decisive action looks like across employment, poverty, skills, and regional inequality. Without this modelling, EU policymakers would be making implementation design decisions without a clear picture of the macro trade-offs.

The Approach

Working at Visionary Analytics as part of an EU-commissioned research team, I contributed to econometric and scenario modelling work projecting outcomes to 2045 across seven EU Member States: Germany, France, Poland, Spain, Italy, Hungary, and Romania. These countries were selected to represent diverse welfare and production regimes across the EU.

The study combined literature review, stakeholder interviews, foresight-based PESTEL scenario building, and partial-equilibrium projections. Three scenarios were developed: Business As Usual (BAU), capturing current policy trajectories without a JTD; Weak JTD implementation, where narrowly scoped measures deliver only limited gains; and Strong JTD implementation, where economy-wide mandates, anticipatory transition planning, and statutory rights to paid training leave are in place.

My contributions included building scenario comparison models in Python, quantifying outcomes across employment, poverty, skills shortages, and regional disparities, and contributing to the written analysis of cross-country spillover effects.

Key Findings

Under the BAU scenario, skills shortages, regional disparities, and pockets of poverty persist or widen as decarbonisation proceeds without coordinated transition support, with the burden falling disproportionately on structurally weak regions.

A strong JTD scenario modestly improves aggregate employment, inequality, and poverty indicators while substantially reducing local dislocation in structurally vulnerable regions, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe.

Because EU economies are tightly integrated, improving transition management in lower-performing countries yields positive spillovers for growth, cohesion, and the political sustainability of climate policy across the bloc.

The weak JTD scenario delivers only limited and localised gains, suggesting that half-measures carry most of the transition costs with few of the benefits, making the case for comprehensive implementation stronger.

Impact

The report was published by the EESC in December 2025 and submitted to EU policymakers as part of the evidence base for Just Transition Directive implementation design. It is publicly available, citable, and part of the formal EU policy record. For the research team, it demonstrated that a robust multi-scenario quantitative model could be delivered within the constraints of an EU research tender.

What I Learned

"Policy modelling is fundamentally about communicating uncertainty honestly. Every scenario projection carries assumptions, and the analyst's job is to make those assumptions visible and explicit. The three-scenario structure exists precisely to resist the temptation of false precision. Three scenarios say: this is the range of what is likely, and here is what determines where you land. That framing is far more useful to a policymaker than a single projection."
